Household preparedness for conflict

Keep everyday life working.

A conflict could cut power, water, phones, fuel or payments here, even with no attack nearby. This site covers what to keep at home, what to do when a service fails, and which official pages to trust.

The aim is to have a fallback ready for each of these if it goes down.

What this is

A guide for Australian households on getting through conflict and long disruptions. Plenty of the practical advice is the same as for floods or fires, and where it is, the source says so. In an actual incident, do what the official instructions say.

01 / The context

What a conflict
could cut off.

War can reach civilians through cyber attacks, shipping delays and failed services, with nothing happening near you. Planning for that doesn't mean expecting it next week.

Know who gives instructions.

States and territories run most crisis responses. Commonwealth agencies coordinate national support and security. Which instructions apply depends on what's happening and where you are.

This site isn't an alert.

It doesn't show live threats, predict attacks or tell you when to evacuate. For that, use the official channels.

02 / Your household

Your household
plan.

Start with three days of supplies, then build up. Go to a week if you can, longer if local guidance or your situation says so. These lengths are my planning choice, not an Australian wartime requirement.

Going past three days.

  • For a week, add more of what you already use: food, water, hygiene and pet supplies. Ask your pharmacist how much medicine to hold.
  • Beyond a week, NSW Health's pantry guide goes up to 14 days at home. That's general emergency advice, not something written for conflict.
  • Whatever length you pick, keep a separate grab bag. A big stock at home is no reason to ignore an evacuation order.

When a service
goes down.

Plan for the outage itself and skip guessing at targets. The steps below come from existing Australian agency guidance. I'm not saying any of these will happen.

04 / Immediate safety

Match the advice
to the emergency.

An armed attacker, an air-attack warning and radioactive contamination call for different responses. Follow what the relevant authority is telling you at the time.

Armed attacker · official Australian advice

Escape. Hide. Tell.

For an attacker with a gun, knife or similar weapon, Australian National Security gives three steps:

  1. Escape quickly and quietly, if it's safe.
  2. Hide if you can't escape safely. Stay out of sight and silence your devices.
  3. Tell police by calling 000 when safe.

Follow police instructions. Don't go closer to watch or film.

This is advice for an armed attacker. It is not a universal shelter rule for bombardment, fallout or any other wartime event.

Radiological incident · specialist direction

Do what the authorities direct.

ARPANSA works on radiation emergency preparedness. Depending on the incident, authorities may tell people to shelter, evacuate or take other protective steps.

  1. If told to shelter, go indoors and follow the instructions you're given.
  2. Use official health advice on food, water, ventilation and when it's safe to leave.
  3. An ordinary building isn't a blast shelter, and there's no fixed number of hours after which you can leave.
Do not self-dose with iodine. ARPANSA says to take potassium iodide only when public authorities advise it. It doesn't protect against radiation in general.
ARPANSA preparedness →Potassium iodide advice →

What about air attacks and public shelters?

I haven't included an air-raid procedure or a list of shelters, because I couldn't verify either from official sources. A basement, underground car park or old wartime shelter isn't automatically a designated or suitable shelter. If an official attack warning goes out, follow its instructions for your location. Don't head for a shelter you've assumed exists, and don't treat overseas advice as an Australian order. In immediate danger, call 000 when safe.

05 / Information

Don't rely on
one source.

A phone alert is one channel, and it can fail. Keep a way to get official information that doesn't need your usual apps or home internet.

Line up a few ways to get information.

Current official instructions

Check police, emergency service, government and health sources for your area. Weather warning apps aren't a complete wartime warning service.

Radio and offline details

Write down local radio frequencies, agency websites and household contacts. A battery radio works without your home internet.

People you trust

Agree on check-ins, especially for anyone living alone or needing help. Check a message before you pass it on.

AusAlert: what we know.

Official material puts the launch of Australia's cell-broadcast warning system in October 2026. NEMA describes states and territories coming on in phases, with the existing SMS system running alongside.

I haven't confirmed that every state and territory is live yet. Check current official information and whether your phone is compatible. If danger is obvious, act. Don't wait for an alert.
